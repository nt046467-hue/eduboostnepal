Where the Confusion Marks Actually Go

Before fixing formula confusions, you need to understand which sections of NEB Physics are most affected — and why getting formulas slightly wrong is more dangerous than not knowing them at all.

Physics is not a memory subject. It's a precision subject.

Students who memorise the right formula but apply it with the wrong sign, wrong variable, or in the wrong context consistently score 2 marks out of 5 on board questions. Knowing a formula 90% correctly is not the same as knowing it. This guide closes the last 10%.

01 · Confusion One

Getting the Doppler Sign Backwards

The Doppler formula has four versions depending on who is moving. Students frequently flip the ± sign, giving a frequency that goes in the wrong direction — and lose all marks for the numerical.

🔊
Confusion 01 · Waves & Sound

Doppler Effect — Wrong ± Sign Choice

01

The Doppler formula is f = f₀ × (v ± v_o) / (v ∓ v_s). The ± symbols depend on the direction of motion. The most common error: students use the same sign for both observer and source, or they flip which sign applies to approach vs recession. This gives a final frequency that is lower when it should be higher — costing the entire numerical.

❌ What students write
f = f₀ × (v − v_o) / (v + v_s)
Used when source approaches — this formula is for source receding. Result: frequency calculated as lower than emitted, but should be higher. Entire answer wrong.
✅ Correct approach formula
f = f₀ × (v + v_o) / (v − v_s)
Numerator: + when observer moves toward source. Denominator: − when source moves toward observer. Approach → frequency increases. Always verify with logic.
❌ Errors students make
  • ✗Applying the same ± for both v_o and v_s
  • ✗Not converting km/h to m/s before substituting
  • ✗Using v_s in numerator instead of denominator
  • ✗Not checking: does answer make physical sense?
✅ The two Doppler rules
  • ✓Moving TOWARD each other → frequency INCREASES
  • ✓Moving AWAY from each other → frequency DECREASES
  • ✓Observer motion: numerator. Source motion: denominator
  • ✓Always do a sanity check on your answer direction
1

Write the complete Doppler formula in one line before substituting. Do not attempt to recall the sign mid-calculation. Write: f = f₀(v ± v_o)/(v ∓ v_s) and label each variable with its value and direction first.

2

Identify the motion direction explicitly. Before touching the formula, write two sentences: "Observer is [moving toward / moving away / stationary]" and "Source is [moving toward / moving away / stationary]." Then apply the sign.

3

Use the physical logic check after every Doppler problem. If source approaches → answer must be > f₀. If source recedes → answer must be < f₀. If your answer violates this, your sign is wrong — not the formula.

Memory Aid — "ODEN": Observer in denominator? No! Observer goes in the Numerator, Source goes in the Denominator. Observer Numerator · Source Denominator. Toward → the sign that makes frequency go UP.
Board tip: In NEB, Doppler problems often involve trains or cars with speeds in km/h. Always convert to m/s as your first written step. Write: "v_s = 72 km/h = 72 × 1000/3600 = 20 m/s." Examiners award a mark for this conversion shown explicitly.
02 · Confusion Two

Using the Wrong Moment of Inertia Formula

NEB uses five different rigid bodies in rotational dynamics questions. Each has its own I formula. Students who don't know which formula to apply — or who confuse disc and sphere — lose all marks for the substitution step.

⚙️
Confusion 02 · Rotational Dynamics

Moment of Inertia — Wrong Body Formula

02

The formula I = ½mR² is not universal. It applies only to a solid disc or solid cylinder about the central axis. A solid sphere uses ⅖mR², a hollow sphere uses ⅔mR², a ring uses mR². Using the wrong formula gives a wrong I, which then cascades into a wrong angular acceleration or wrong kinetic energy — causing multiple mark losses from one error.

❌ Most confused pairs
  • ✗Using ½mR² for a solid sphere (should be ⅖mR²)
  • ✗Using mR² for a disc (that's the ring formula)
  • ✗Confusing hollow sphere (⅔) with solid sphere (⅖)
  • ✗Applying axis-formula without reading which axis is specified
✅ The 5 formulas — memorise all
  • ✓Ring (thin): I = mR²
  • ✓Disc / Solid cylinder: I = ½mR²
  • ✓Solid sphere: I = ⅖mR²
  • ✓Hollow sphere (thin shell): I = ⅔mR²
  • ✓Hollow cylinder: I = m(R₁²+R₂²)/2
1

Write the five MI formulas as a single row at the top of every rotational dynamics solution. In the exam, write this row before substituting. Examiners see it as a formula statement — it earns its own mark and prevents the wrong one being used.

2

Notice the fraction pattern: Fractions increase as mass is distributed farther from the axis. Ring (all mass at rim) → 1. Hollow sphere (mass near surface) → ⅔. Solid cylinder (mass spread inward) → ½. Solid sphere (most mass near centre) → ⅖.

3

For rolling body problems, always write both KE_translational = ½mv² AND KE_rotational = ½Iω² separately, then add. Never try to combine them in your head — the error always happens when steps are skipped.

Memory trick — "1, ½, ⅖, ⅔": Sort bodies from most hollow to most solid. Ring (completely hollow, mass on rim) = 1. Hollow sphere = ⅔. Disc/cylinder = ½. Solid sphere = ⅖. The more solid/filled the body, the smaller the fraction — mass is closer to the axis, so less resistance to rotation.
Board tip: NEB questions often say "a disc rolls without slipping." This means both translational and rotational KE must be included. Students who only write ½mv² get 1 out of 4 marks. Always check the word "rolling" — that is the trigger for using both kinetic energy terms.
03 · Confusion Three

Getting Thermodynamics Signs Wrong

Thermodynamics has two classic confusion points: the sign convention in the First Law (ΔU = Q − W vs ΔU = Q + W), and using Celsius instead of Kelvin for efficiency. Both are silent killers — everything looks right, but the answer is wrong.

🔥
Confusion 03 · Thermodynamics

First Law Sign & Temperature Unit Errors

03

There are two separate confusion points here. First: ΔU = Q − W (physics convention, W = work done BY the gas) vs ΔU = Q + W (chemistry convention, W = work done ON the gas). NEB Physics uses ΔU = Q − W. Second: Carnot efficiency η = 1 − T₂/T₁ requires T in Kelvin. Using 27°C and 327°C directly gives a completely wrong percentage — yet students do this repeatedly under exam pressure.

❌ Common wrong versions
η = 1 − 27/327 = 91.7%
Used raw Celsius temperatures. This is physically impossible — a 92% efficient heat engine violates thermodynamic limits. But students write it confidently every year.
✅ Correct — always convert first
T₁ = 327+273=600K, T₂ = 27+273=300K
η = 1 − 300/600 = 50%
Convert BOTH temperatures before writing any formula. This is non-negotiable. Write the Kelvin values as your first step — not after.
❌ What students confuse
  • ✗ΔU = Q + W (chemistry sign, wrong in NEB Physics)
  • ✗Using °C directly in η = 1 − T₂/T₁
  • ✗Treating work done ON the gas as positive in ΔU = Q − W
  • ✗Forgetting that isothermal expansion: ΔU = 0, so Q = W
✅ NEB Physics conventions
  • ✓ΔU = Q − W where W = work done BY gas (NEB standard)
  • ✓Heat absorbed: Q is positive. Heat released: Q is negative
  • ✓Work done BY gas: W positive. Work done ON gas: W negative
  • ✓Kelvin always. Add 273 to BOTH temperatures, always
1

Write "T₁ = ___°C + 273 = ___ K" as your literal first written line in every thermodynamics problem that gives temperature in Celsius. Make it a reflex. Not a reminder — an automatic habit that fires before the formula is even written.

2

For First Law problems, label each quantity with its sign before substituting. Write: "Q = +800 J (absorbed), W = +300 J (done by gas), ΔU = ?" Then apply ΔU = Q − W. This forces you to assign signs consciously rather than mechanically.

3

Sanity check for Carnot efficiency: η must always be between 0 and 1 (i.e., 0% to 100%). An answer above 100% means you used Celsius — go back and convert. An answer above 80% for typical NEB temperatures is suspicious and worth checking.

Memory aid — "KABK": Kelvin Always, Both temperatures, Karnot. Any time you see Carnot or efficiency, recite "KABK" and convert both T values before writing a single formula character.
Board tip: In NEB 2079, a full 5-mark Carnot question was lost by over 40% of students because of the Celsius error. The question gave 327°C and 27°C. Students who wrote T₁ = 600K and T₂ = 300K got full marks in under 3 minutes. Students who skipped conversion answered 91.7% and scored 0 on the numerical portion.
04 · Confusion Four

Inconsistent Sign Convention in Optics

Optics numericals in NEB follow the New Cartesian Sign Convention. Students who mix up which distances are positive or negative — or who use the mirror formula for a lens — lose all steps after the formula line.

🔭
Confusion 04 · Optics

Lens & Mirror Sign Convention Errors

04

The New Cartesian Sign Convention has simple rules: all distances measured from the pole/optical centre. Distances in the direction of incident light (usually left to right) are positive. Against incident light is negative. Object distance u is always negative for real objects. Students either forget u is negative, mix up the lens and mirror formula forms, or use f as negative for a convex lens.

❌ Lens formula mistakes
1/v + 1/u = 1/f (wrong form)
f = −20 cm for convex lens ✗
The correct lens formula is 1/v − 1/u = 1/f. Adding instead of subtracting is the single most common NEB optics error. A convex lens has positive f.
✅ Correct sign rules
1/v − 1/u = 1/f (lens, always)
1/v + 1/u = 1/f (mirror, always)
Lens: subtract. Mirror: add. Convex lens → f positive. Concave lens → f negative. Concave mirror → f negative. Convex mirror → f positive.
❌ Most confused sign rules
  • ✗Object distance u taken as positive (u is always −ve for real object)
  • ✗Convex lens focal length taken as negative
  • ✗Using mirror formula 1/v + 1/u = 1/f for a lens question
  • ✗Magnification sign: m = v/u written without the negative
✅ NEB sign convention table
  • ✓u = always negative (real object, left of lens)
  • ✓Convex lens/mirror: f positive. Concave: f negative
  • ✓Real image: v positive (right of lens). Virtual: v negative
  • ✓Magnification m = v/u. Real inverted image → m negative
1

Write the sign convention table before every optics solution. Literally write three lines: "u = −(value), f = +(value) [convex], Formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f [lens]." This takes 20 seconds and eliminates the most common optics error class entirely.

2

Remember "Lens Minus, Mirror Plus." The lens formula has a minus between the two fractions. The mirror formula has a plus. This single distinction separates two formulas that look nearly identical but produce different answers every time.

3

Check your magnification answer physically. If the question says "real inverted image," m must be negative. If the question says "virtual erect image," m must be positive. A wrong sign on m means your v has the wrong sign — go back and check.

Memory trick — "LM Rule": Lens has a Minus (1/v − 1/u). Mirror has a Plus (1/v + 1/u = 1/f). LM = Lens Minus. Say it before writing every optics formula.
Board tip: Magnification questions in NEB almost always specify whether the image is real or virtual, erect or inverted. These words are sign information. "Real, inverted, 3× magnified" tells you m = −3. Write this as the very first line of your solution: "m = −3 (real, inverted)." Examiners give a mark for identifying m correctly.
05 · Confusion Five

Swapping ρ and σ in Stokes' Law

Stokes' Law for terminal velocity has two density terms: ρ (density of the falling body) and σ (density of the fluid). Swapping them gives a negative or nonsensically large velocity — and the full numerical is lost.

💧
Confusion 05 · Fluid Mechanics

Stokes' Law — Density Variable Swap

05

The terminal velocity formula is v_t = 2r²(ρ − σ)g / 9η, where ρ is the density of the sphere (falling body) and σ is the density of the fluid (medium). Students who write v_t = 2r²(σ − ρ)g / 9η get a negative velocity when the body is denser than the fluid — which is physically meaningless. The common confusion: the fluid is mentioned more prominently in the question context, so students associate it with ρ instead of σ.

❌ Swapped densities
v_t = 2r²(σ − ρ)g / 9η
σ is fluid, ρ is the body. Steel ball in oil: ρ_steel = 7800, σ_oil = 900. This gives a large negative number. Student realises something is wrong, panics, and wastes time.
✅ Correct formula
v_t = 2r²(ρ − σ)g / 9η
ρ = density of the falling sphere. σ = density of the surrounding fluid. (ρ − σ) must be positive for downward terminal velocity. If (ρ − σ) is negative, the body floats upward.
❌ What students get wrong
  • ✗ρ assigned to fluid (glycerine, water, oil) instead of the falling body
  • ✗Radius not converted from mm to m (factor of 10⁻³, then squared → 10⁻⁶)
  • ✗Forgetting to square the radius r² in the formula
  • ✗Using diameter instead of radius
✅ Correct application steps
  • ✓ρ = density of falling object. σ = density of fluid. Label these first.
  • ✓Convert radius: mm → m. Write: r = __ mm = __×10⁻³ m
  • ✓r² = (value)² = written out explicitly before substituting
  • ✓Check: (ρ − σ) must be positive. If not, verify which is which.
1

Write ρ and σ labels immediately after Given. "ρ (sphere) = 7800 kg/m³, σ (fluid) = 900 kg/m³." This takes 5 seconds. It forces you to assign the correct variable to the correct quantity before any formula is touched.

2

Always check the sign of (ρ − σ) before computing. If the ball is denser than the fluid (steel, iron, glass in water/oil), ρ > σ, so the result must be positive. If you get a negative, you swapped the densities.

3

Don't forget: radius must be in metres, and it is squared. A 2 mm ball: r = 2×10⁻³ m, r² = 4×10⁻⁶ m². Students who forget to square or forget the unit conversion get an answer off by a factor of 10⁶ — clearly wrong but often not caught under time pressure.

Memory trick — "ρ is the Particle, σ is the Sea": ρ = the Particle/ball falling. σ = the Surrounding fluid/sea. When you see the Stokes formula (ρ − σ), read it as "Particle minus Sea." Particle density minus fluid density. Always that order.
Board tip: NEB Stokes questions always give you a ball (steel, lead, glass) falling through a viscous fluid (glycerine, oil, castor oil). The ball is always denser. So ρ (ball) is always the BIGGER number. If you've assigned ρ as the smaller number, you've swapped them — stop and swap back.
06 · Confusion Six

Mixing Up Photoelectric Quantities

The photoelectric equation has three linked quantities: photon energy (E = hf), work function (φ), and maximum kinetic energy (KE_max). Students confuse which is which, mix up units between joules and eV, and forget the stopping potential relationship.

⚛️
Confusion 06 · Modern Physics

Photoelectric Equation — Variable & Unit Mix-up

06

Einstein's photoelectric equation: KE_max = hf − φ. Three quantities, and students confuse all three in various ways. The most damaging: calculating photon energy in joules, the work function is given in eV, and the student subtracts them directly without converting. This gives a nonsensical answer every time. The second confusion: stopping potential V₀ is not the same as KE_max — the relation is eV₀ = KE_max.

❌ Classic unit mismatch
KE_max = 4.97×10⁻¹⁹ − 2.0 = ???
Student computed photon energy as 4.97×10⁻¹⁹ J but work function φ is given as 2.0 eV. Subtracting J from eV directly gives a meaningless result. This destroys the entire numerical.
✅ Correct: unify units first
E = 4.97×10⁻¹⁹ J ÷ 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ = 3.1 eV
KE_max = 3.1 − 2.0 = 1.1 eV
V₀ = 1.1 eV / e = 1.1 V
Convert photon energy to eV immediately. Then all three quantities are in eV and subtraction is valid. Stopping potential numerically equals KE_max in eV.
❌ Three fatal errors
  • ✗Subtracting joules from eV (unit mismatch, answer meaningless)
  • ✗Confusing work function φ with photon energy E (φ is a property of the metal; E depends on incident light)
  • ✗Writing V₀ = KE_max numerically without understanding eV₀ = KE_max(joules)
  • ✗Using wavelength in nm directly without converting to metres
✅ Three-step process
  • ✓Step 1: E = hc/λ (convert λ to metres first)
  • ✓Step 2: Convert E to eV (divide by 1.6×10⁻¹⁹)
  • ✓Step 3: KE_max = E − φ (both now in eV). V₀ = KE_max value in volts
  • ✓Sanity check: KE_max must be positive. If negative, light is below threshold.
1

Always work entirely in eV for photoelectric problems. Convert photon energy to eV immediately after calculating E = hc/λ. Write "E = __ J = __ eV" as a single combined line. From this point on, all calculations are in eV. Never mix units mid-solution.

2

Understand the three quantities physically: φ is a fixed property of the metal — it doesn't change no matter what light you shine. E is the energy of the incoming photon — it depends on frequency/wavelength. KE_max is what's left after the photon "pays" for the electron to escape. E − φ = what's left over.

3

Stopping potential trick: If KE_max = 1.1 eV, then V₀ = 1.1 V. The numerical value is identical. This is because eV₀ = KE_max, so V₀ = KE_max(in eV) / 1 electron charge. The eV unit was designed for exactly this — eV₀ = KE_max cancels beautifully.

Memory chain — "Photon Pays Tax": Photon arrives with energy E (= hc/λ). It pays a Tax = work function φ to let the electron escape. What's left = KE_max = E − φ. The stopping voltage = the amount of "energy left" expressed in volts. If photon can't pay the tax (E < φ), no emission at all.
Board tip: NEB almost always gives wavelength in nm and work function in eV. Convert wavelength to metres (×10⁻⁹) and compute E in joules, then immediately convert to eV. Students who skip this conversion or defer it to "do later" almost always forget — resulting in a subtraction that is physically impossible.
07 · Confusion Seven

Getting Kirchhoff KVL Signs Wrong

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law problems carry 6–8 marks in NEB — the highest single numerical value in the paper. The confusion is not in setting up the loops, but in the sign of EMF and voltage drop terms as you traverse each loop.

⚡
Confusion 07 · Electricity · Highest Risk

Kirchhoff KVL — Loop Traversal Sign Errors

07

The most marks-dense confusion in NEB Physics. KVL says the sum of all EMFs and voltage drops around a closed loop equals zero. The sign of each term depends on the traversal direction relative to the assumed current direction and battery polarity. Students who don't have a consistent rule write loop equations that are partially or fully wrong — and the simultaneous equations that follow give completely wrong currents.

❌ Random sign assignment
E₁ + E₂ − I₁R₁ − I₂R₂ = 0
Student included both EMFs as positive without checking traversal direction relative to battery polarity. If the traversal goes from + to − through one battery, that EMF is actually negative in the loop equation.
✅ Systematic KVL rule
Traverse − to + through battery → +E
Traverse + to − through battery → −E
Traverse against assumed I through R → +IR
Traverse with assumed I through R → −IR
Apply this rule mechanically around every loop. Never assign signs by intuition — always check traversal direction against each element's orientation.
❌ Most common KVL errors
  • ✗Both EMFs taken as positive regardless of traversal direction
  • ✗Forgetting internal resistance r in the loop equation
  • ✗Not applying KCL at junction before writing KVL loops
  • ✗Solving for I and getting a negative, then panicking and ignoring the sign
  • ✗Writing two loop equations that are not independent (same loop twice)
✅ Systematic KVL approach
  • ✓Label all branch currents with arrows BEFORE writing equations
  • ✓Apply KCL at every junction first: sum of currents in = sum out
  • ✓Choose traversal direction for each loop (clockwise is fine)
  • ✓A negative I answer just means current flows opposite to your assumed direction — report magnitude with corrected direction
  • ✓Include internal resistance r as part of the series resistance
1

Always draw the circuit before writing equations. Even if the question doesn't require a diagram, sketch it yourself. Label each branch with an assumed current direction and an arrow. This visual reference prevents sign errors in the loop traversal by making the direction explicit.

2

Apply the consistent traversal rule to each element one at a time. As you trace your loop clockwise, pause at each element: "Am I going − to + through this battery? → +E." "Am I going in the same direction as assumed current through this resistor? → −IR." Do it element by element, never all at once.

3

A negative current answer is not a mistake. If you solve and get I₂ = −1.5 A, that means 1.5 A flows in the direction opposite to what you assumed. Write: "I₂ = 1.5 A (direction reversed)." Students who erase their equations and redo everything when they get a negative value waste 10 minutes and often introduce new errors.

KVL traversal rule — just two cases to remember:
Going through a battery: Against the current inside (− to +) → EMF is positive.
Going through a resistor: Against the assumed current → voltage term is positive.
Think of it as: "If I'm fighting against something, I gain voltage (+). If I'm going with the flow, I lose voltage (−)."
Board tip: NEB KVL problems with two batteries and three resistors are worth 8 marks and take up to 15 minutes if you make sign errors. Students who get the loop equations right in one attempt finish in 8 minutes and pick up all marks. Practice writing loop equations for at least 10 different circuit configurations before the board exam — not just solving them, but writing the equations correctly on the first attempt.
